AWS runs data centres all over the world, organised in layers: **Regions** contain **Availability Zones**, and **edge locations** sit even closer to users. Knowing how these fit together is the basis for availability and latency questions.

## Regions

A **Region** is a separate **geographic area**, such as `eu-west-2` (London) or `us-east-1` (N. Virginia). Regions are **isolated** from each other, and **your data doesn't leave a Region unless you move it**.

### Four factors for choosing a Region

1. **Compliance / data governance:** e.g. data must stay in the UK or the EU. Usually the **deciding** factor.
2. **Proximity to customers:** closer means lower latency.
3. **Service availability:** new services don't launch in every Region at once.
4. **Pricing:** costs differ between Regions.

## Availability Zones (AZs)

An **Availability Zone** is **one or more discrete data centres** with their own redundant power, networking and connectivity, **inside a Region**. Most Regions have **three or more** AZs, physically separated (miles apart) but linked by fast, low-latency networking.

```
Region: eu-west-2 (London)
 ├── AZ eu-west-2a  [data centre][data centre]
 ├── AZ eu-west-2b  [data centre]
 └── AZ eu-west-2c  [data centre][data centre]
```

**Best practice: run across at least two AZs**, so a fire, flood or power cut in one AZ doesn't take your application down. That's **high availability**.

Some services are **regional** and automatically spread across AZs for you: S3, DynamoDB, Elastic Load Balancing, Lambda.

## Edge locations

**Edge locations** (Points of Presence) are hundreds of smaller sites around the world, far more than there are Regions. They bring content close to users and are used by:

- **Amazon CloudFront:** the content delivery network (CDN). It **caches** copies of content (images, video, web pages, APIs) at edge locations, so users get them from nearby.
- **Amazon Route 53:** DNS (Module 5).

## Getting even closer

| Service | What it is | Use for |
|---|---|---|
| **AWS Global Accelerator** | Routes users onto the AWS global network via fixed (anycast) IP addresses | Faster, more reliable paths for global apps. **No caching** |
| **AWS Local Zones** | AWS compute and storage placed in large cities | Single-digit-millisecond latency (media, gaming) |
| **AWS Wavelength Zones** | AWS infrastructure inside 5G telecom networks | Ultra-low-latency mobile apps |
| **AWS Outposts** | AWS racks in your own data centre | On-premises needs (Module 3) |

## Exam traps

- An AZ is **not** a single data centre. It's **one or more** data centres.
- **CloudFront caches**; Global Accelerator **doesn't** (it improves the network path).
- To survive a data-centre failure: **multiple AZs**. To survive a whole-Region disaster: **multiple Regions**.

## Check yourself

1. A company must keep customer data inside the EU. Which factor drives its Region choice?
2. What's the best practice for making an application highly available?
3. Which service caches content at edge locations close to users?

### Answers

1. Compliance / data governance.
2. Deploy it across at least two Availability Zones.
3. Amazon CloudFront.
